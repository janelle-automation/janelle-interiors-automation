Below are the highest-value AI prompts an interior design firm can standardize across its team. I would treat these as your internal prompt library for Janelle Interiors.
1. Full Project Design Direction
Act as a senior interior designer at a luxury residential and hospitality design firm.

Review the client brief, architectural plans, inspiration images, existing conditions, budget, and project constraints.

Develop a cohesive design direction for the entire project.

For each room, provide:

- Overall design concept
- Color palette
- Material direction
- Flooring
- Wall finishes
- Cabinetry / millwork direction
- Countertop and stone direction
- Plumbing finish
- Lighting direction
- Furniture style
- Textile direction
- Key architectural details
- Connections to adjacent rooms
- Items requiring client approval
- Information still missing

Maintain one cohesive design language throughout the project while allowing each room to have its own identity.

Do not invent dimensions, products, or specifications. Mark missing information as TBD.

2. Room-by-Room Moodboard Prompt
Act as a senior interior designer.

Develop a moodboard direction for:

CLIENT:
ROOM:
PROJECT STYLE:
BUDGET LEVEL:
EXISTING CONDITIONS:
CLIENT REQUESTS:

Create a clear design concept including:

- 5–8 inspiration directions
- Overall color palette
- Tile / stone direction
- Wood tone
- Metal finish
- Cabinetry direction
- Wall finish
- Lighting style
- Furniture direction
- Textile direction
- Architectural details

Explain how the selections work together.

The moodboard should establish design direction only. Do not treat inspiration images as actual product selections.

3. Pinterest Research Prompt
Act as an interior design research assistant.

I am designing a [ROOM] with the following direction:

[DESIGN DESCRIPTION]

Create a targeted Pinterest research list.

Give me specific search phrases for:

- Overall room inspiration
- Tile layouts
- Tile patterns
- Cabinetry
- Millwork
- Stone
- Lighting
- Plumbing
- Furniture
- Architectural details
- Color palette

Avoid generic search terms.

Give me highly specific Pinterest searches that will produce professional designer-level inspiration.

7. Interior Elevation Prompt
Act as a senior interior designer and interior architectural designer.

Review the architectural plans and approved finish selections for this room.

Create an interior elevation planning brief showing exactly what needs to be documented.

Include:

- Overall wall dimensions
- Ceiling height
- Cabinetry
- Millwork
- Countertops
- Backsplashes
- Tile extents
- Tile direction
- Plumbing fixtures
- Mirrors
- Decorative lighting
- Niches
- Shelving
- Hardware
- Artwork
- Furniture if applicable
- Electrical coordination
- Mounting heights
- Key dimensions

Identify any dimensions or architectural information missing before drafting begins.

The finished elevation should answer:

WHAT GOES WHERE?

8. Elevation Quality-Control Prompt
Act as a senior interior designer reviewing a junior designer's interior elevation.

Review the elevation for:

- Accuracy
- Proportion
- Missing dimensions
- Tile placement
- Tile termination
- Centerlines
- Plumbing alignment
- Lighting alignment
- Mirror placement
- Cabinetry proportions
- Countertop thickness
- Hardware
- Niches
- Trim
- Material callouts
- Constructability
- Coordination with architectural plans

Identify:

1. Errors
2. Missing information
3. Items requiring GC confirmation
4. Items requiring Lead Designer approval
5. Recommended corrections

Do not approve the drawing if critical construction information is missing.

12. Client Meeting Preparation

- Items to present
- Items requiring client approval
- Alternatives to discuss
- Budget concerns
- Lead-time concerns
- Design questions
- Missing information

14. Missing-Information Audit
This is one of the most useful prompts for junior designers.
Act as a senior interior designer performing a completeness audit.

Review the entire project.

For every room identify missing:

- Moodboards
- Material selections
- Finish selections
- Plumbing
- Lighting
- Flooring
- Paint
- Wallpaper
- Cabinet finishes
- Hardware
- Elevations
- Dimensions
- FF&E
- Client approvals
- Samples
- Vendor quotes
- GC information
- Architect information

Create a punch list organized:

RED — Blocking progress
YELLOW — Needed soon
GREEN — Can wait

15. Client Approval Audit
Review the project and identify the approval status of every material and FF&E selection.

Sort into:

APPROVED

WAITING FOR CLIENT

WAITING FOR LEAD DESIGNER

RESELECT

TBD

NOT YET PRESENTED

For each unresolved item state exactly what action needs to happen next.

17. Designer Hours Review
Act as the COO of an interior design firm.

Review the designer's time spent on this project.

Compare actual hours against standard target hours for:

- Project setup
- Moodboards
- Material sourcing
- Plumbing
- Lighting
- Elevations
- Finish Schedule
- FF&E sourcing
- FF&E Schedule
- Canva updates
- Client revisions
- Coordination

Identify:

- Appropriate hours
- Excessive hours
- Work that should have been delegated
- Rework
- Client-driven revisions
- Scope creep
- Opportunities to improve efficiency

Finish with recommended hours remaining to complete the project.

18. Weekly Lead Designer Project Review
Act as the Lead Designer and COO.

Review the entire project and tell me:

- Current design phase
- Percent design complete
- Rooms complete
- Rooms incomplete
- Client approvals outstanding
- Lead Designer approvals outstanding
- Missing elevations
- Missing materials
- Missing FF&E
- Missing Finish Schedule information
- Missing vendor quotes
- GC decisions needed
- Long-lead risks
- Designer tasks overdue
- Procurement tasks overdue

Then create the top 10 priorities for the design team this week.

The 5 prompts I would require your team to use every week
If you want the system simple, make these mandatory:
Missing-Information Audit
Design Presentation Review
Interior Elevation QC
Procurement Readiness Audit
Weekly Lead Designer Project Review
Those five prompts can catch a large percentage of the issues that normally cause interior-design projects to fall behind: unfinished selections, unclear approvals, incomplete drawings, missing specifications, and procurement not knowing what is actually ready to order.
